the lyric descriptors and the caption are two separate conditioning streams that the adapter reads during training, and both are subject to the same rule.
The Two Conditioning Streams in LoKR Training
Stream	What it conditions	What the adapter learns from it
Caption	Global sound portrait	"When these words appear, produce this overall sound"
Lyrics + section descriptors	Temporal script	"When this section marker and these descriptors appear, produce this specific moment"

Both streams are active during training. The adapter adjusts its weights based on both. If a descriptor appears in every solo bracket across your dataset, the adapter learns that descriptor is a cause of the AC/DC solo sound — exactly the same leakage problem as putting "hard rock" in every caption.
The Rule Applied to Lyric Descriptors

If a descriptor describes something constant across your entire dataset → omit it from every bracket.

If a descriptor describes something that varies between songs or within a song → include it.
Omit from Every Bracket (Constant AC/DC Bon Scott Vocabulary)

These appear in every song and define the band's sound. They must be absorbed by your trigger tag, not written in brackets.

Vocal descriptors (constant Bon Scott delivery):
raspy vocal, sneering, snarling, gritty, swaggering, bluesy shout, high-pitched snarl, taunting, whooping, wailing

Solo descriptors (constant Angus Young vocabulary):
blues-based leads, pentatonic, vibrato, bend, pull-off, hammer-on, double-stop, alternate picking, melodic, vocal-like phrasing

Production/energy descriptors (constant AC/DC aesthetic):
dry, raw production, live feel, punchy, in-your-face, no frills, high energy, driving, relentless, anthemic

Instrument focus (constant):
guitar solo, riff-based, power chords, palm-muted riffs, driving rhythm guitar
Include in Brackets (Variable Elements)

These describe what makes this specific section or this specific song different.

Vocal variation within a song:

    [Verse 1 - whispered, menacing] (if this verse is whispered — e.g., "Night Prowler")

    [Chorus - shouted, gang vocals] (if this chorus has a specific shouted delivery)

    [Bridge - spoken word] (if this section departs from singing)

    [Verse 2 - building, strained] (if this verse escalates)

Solo variation per song:

    [Guitar Solo - wah-wah engaged, short stabbed phrases] ("Whole Lotta Rosie")

    [Guitar Solo - slow sustained bends, dark tone] ("Night Prowler")

    [Guitar Solo - clean tone, restrained, melodic] ("Ride On")

    [Guitar Solo - extended, building to frenzied alternate picking] ("Let There Be Rock")

Structural variation:

    [Intro - solo guitar riff, no drums] (if this song opens that way)

    [Breakdown - bass and drums only] (if this song has that)

    [Outro - fade out, repeated riff] (if this song ends that way)

Dynamic variation:

    [Verse 1 - sparse, quiet] (if this verse is notably restrained)

    [Chorus - explosive, full band] (if the dynamic contrast is a feature of this song)

Concrete Example: "Whole Lotta Rosie" vs "Night Prowler"
Wrong Way (leakage — constant descriptors in every bracket)
text

[Intro - driving guitar riff, tight drums]

[Verse 1 - raspy vocal, sneering, sparse]

[Chorus - anthemic, full band, high energy]

[Guitar Solo - blues-based leads, pentatonic, vibrato]

[Outro - fade out, raucous, relentless]

The adapter learns: "raspy vocal, sneering, anthemic, blues-based leads" → AC/DC sound. Your trigger tag becomes redundant. At inference, writing "raspy vocal, sneering, blues-based leads" without the trigger still produces AC/DC-like output. The trigger is not a clean switch.
Right Way (omission — only variable elements in brackets)

"Whole Lotta Rosie":
text

[Intro - solo guitar riff, no drums]

[Verse 1 - sparse, vocal front and center]

[Chorus - shouted gang vocal on title phrase, full band]

[Guitar Solo - short stabbed phrases, wah-wah engaged, call-and-response with rhythm guitar]

[Outro - abrupt cut]

"Night Prowler":
text

[Intro - lone guitar figure, no drums]

[Verse 1 - whispered, menacing, building tension]

[Chorus - slow heavy release, snarling title phrase]

[Guitar Solo - slow sustained bends, dark tone, sparse]

[Outro - fade out on repeated riff]

Now the adapter learns: "When the trigger tag is present in the caption, produce AC/DC Bon Scott style. Within that style, 'whispered, menacing' means whispered and menacing; 'wah-wah engaged, short stabbed phrases' means that specific solo character."

The trigger absorbs the constant vocabulary. The brackets describe only the temporal variation.
Why This Matters Specifically for Solos

Angus Young's solo vocabulary is consistent enough that if you write "blues-based leads, pentatonic, vibrato" in every solo bracket, the adapter will learn those words as the cause of the AC/DC solo sound. Then at inference, someone writing "blues-based leads, pentatonic, vibrato" with the trigger off still gets a semi-AC/DC solo. The trigger fails as a switch.

The specific solo character — wah-wah, slow bends, frenzied alternate picking, call-and-response with rhythm guitar — is what varies between songs. That variation is what belongs in the bracket.
The One-Sentence Rule (Expanded)

    If a descriptor appears in every caption AND every bracket across your dataset, it must appear in neither. The trigger tag is the only name for that descriptor.

SFT-Specific Note

SFT preserves the alignment logic that makes the bracket descriptors temporally precise. This means:

    SFT can learn from nuanced bracket descriptors (e.g., "call-and-response with rhythm guitar").

    Turbo cannot, because its distillation removed that alignment logic.

    Base can, but Base is for special tasks (lego/complete/extract), not LoKR fine-tuning.

Since you are using SFT, you can trust that the bracket descriptors you write will be interpreted as temporal modifiers — which is exactly what you want for song-specific solo and vocal variation.
